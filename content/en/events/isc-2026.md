  <td class="author w-70"> <b><a href="#Yuji_Sanada">Yuji Sanada</a></b></a></a><br>(Mitsubishi Electric)<br>
   Hidden Barriers to InnerSource
  <span onClick="toggleAbstract('Yuji_Sanada_abstract')" class="abstract-toggle">(<a id="Yuji_Sanada-link">Show Abstract</a>)</span>
     <div style="display:none" class="abstract" id=Yuji_Sanada_abstract>

In 2025, Mitsubishi Electric introduced the InnerSource Value Co-Creation Model (IVCM) at InnerSource Summit. Our message was that InnerSource should go beyond code sharing. By sharing not only implementations, but also approaches and challenges across business domains, organizations can create value even when direct software reuse is difficult.

We also introduced an InnerSource platform model based on Discovery, Connection, and Collaboration, supported by technologies such as GitHub Enterprise, Backstage, and generative AI.

As we continued to expand InnerSource across a conglomerate, however, we encountered a more fundamental issue: valuable information is not always disclosed in the first place.

Even when engineers are willing to share and the technical platform is available, information may remain inside organizational boundaries. In a conglomerate, different business domains have different customers, regulations, business practices, decision processes, and priorities. These differences can create barriers that make organization-wide disclosure and reuse difficult.

Rather than assuming why information was not being disclosed, we turned to our InnerSource community itself. We raised the issue openly and collected feedback through surveys and interviews. This helped us identify recurring barriers behind seemingly unrelated concerns from different business domains.

We then connected these findings to Mitsubishi Electric's company-wide Data Utilization Statement, which calls for data to be interconnected and circulated across businesses and organizational boundaries. Instead of treating these barriers as isolated InnerSource problems, we reframed them as structural issues that could prevent the company from realizing its stated direction and brought them to management.

Today, we are beginning to work with legal and other corporate functions to explore how these barriers should be addressed. We do not yet have complete solutions, and the barriers themselves continue to evolve as we deepen our analysis.

In this session, we will briefly revisit the IVCM vision introduced in 2025 and share what we learned from moving that vision into practice: what kinds of barriers emerge when InnerSource is expanded across a conglomerate, how we used the InnerSource community itself to identify those barriers, and how connecting them to corporate strategy helped us bring the issue to management and relevant corporate functions.

To scale InnerSource value co-creation, organizations need to understand what prevents information from crossing organizational boundaries and elevate those barriers from local concerns to enterprise-wide issues.

  <td class="author w-70"> <b><a href="#Mishari_Muqbil">Mishari Muqbil</a></b><br>(Mishari.net)<br>
   Scaling Context, Not Code
  <span onClick="toggleAbstract('Mishari_Muqbil_abstract')" class="abstract-toggle">(<a id="Mishari_Muqbil-link">Show Abstract</a>)</span>
     <div style="display:none" class="abstract" id=Mishari_Muqbil_abstract>

As AI makes code cheaper to produce, software teams need better ways to share context, judgment, and trust.

This talk starts with a long-serving maintainer who had become the person everyone relied on to explain why the system worked the way it did. When he prepared to retire, the team faced a familiar problem. Years of architectural reasoning, past decisions, exceptions, and unwritten knowledge lived largely in one person's head.

We captured his conversations, guidance, and technical documentation so AI could use them. The AI became a first place for developers to ask questions about the system. It could point people to past decisions, explain architectural choices, and help contributors understand the code before they asked a maintainer for help.

This experience changed how I think about InnerSource in an AI-heavy development environment.

InnerSource already creates much of the context that AI needs. Shared architecture, documentation, contribution rules, ownership, decision records, and working practices help people understand how a system should evolve. They also give AI enough context to reason about intent and constraints instead of working from source code alone.

AI also changes the volume of work flowing through these systems. Developers can create changes much faster, while trusted committers still need to understand the consequences of those changes. Review, judgment, and trust become harder to scale.

This talk looks at how InnerSource can help teams make shared context available to both people and AI, preserve knowledge when experienced engineers leave, reduce avoidable mistakes, and adapt the trusted committer role as AI becomes part of the team.

  <td class="author w-70"> <b><a href="#Sarah_Novotny">Sarah Novotny</a></b><br>(No Kat Food Limited)<br>
   Free Like a Puppy, Fast Like a Robot: Trust When the Contributor Isn't Human
  <span onClick="toggleAbstract('Sarah_Novotny_abstract')" class="abstract-toggle">(<a id="Sarah_Novotny-link">Show Abstract</a>)</span>
    <div style="display:none" class="abstract" id=Sarah_Novotny_abstract>

I've spent two decades watching open source run on a currency our dashboards never capture: trust. InnerSource inherited that currency. The Trusted Committer, the host-team handshake, the willingness to review a patch from three orgs over, these are trust instruments. We accrue them slowly, and right now we are spending them down on two fronts at once.

The first front is provenance. A pull request used to imply a person. Someone who reasoned about the change, who can be asked why, who will still be here to answer for it. When an agent authors the diff, that chain quietly severs. No one vouches, and no one carries the cost of a bad vouch. The code merges anyway: plausible, well-formatted, and unowned. The bill arrives later, all at once, as maintenance debt no human in the room can explain. This is free like a puppy, deferred a year. The code is free today; the care comes due exactly when you can least afford it.

The second front is the pipeline, and it is the one your finance team is actively mispricing. The starter issues and low-risk pull requests that juniors once cut their teeth on are precisely the work we now hand to agents. That work was never only output. It was how a contributor became someone the community could trust to say yes. Automate the apprenticeship away and you don't only lose junior throughput; you lose the mechanism that produces next year's Trusted Committers. The trust network you have on Thursday is the review bandwidth you have when Friday's flood of machine-authored contributions lands. Starve the pipeline, and that bandwidth simply isn't there. You won't notice until you reach for it.

So this is not a talk about slowing AI down. It is a talk about who honors the 30-Day Warranty when the author is a model, and where the humans who can answer that come from. Open source learned this the expensive way: software is people. The answer to trust has always been people, specifically the ones we deliberately grow into the roles that hold it.

I'll offer a working frame built on provenance, vouching, and trust as bandwidth built before you need it, plus three concrete moves: treat AI provenance as a first-class review signal; make no sudden moves with the norms your contributors rely on; and fund early-career pathways as apprenticeship, not probation, because that pipeline is trust infrastructure, not headcount you can defer.

Trust is the ultimate currency. In the AI era, InnerSource is where enterprises mint it, or quietly go insolvent.

**Yoshitake Kobayashi** (InnerSource in the Age of AI: Rediscovering the "Source" in InnerSource)


Yoshitake Kobayashi is the Assistant General Manager and Senior Fellow of the Digital Innovation Technology Center at Toshiba Corporation, where his team delivers a Linux distribution for a wide range of Toshiba products. Since the earliest days of InnerSource in Japan, he has been among those introducing the practice to Japanese industry, and has put it into practice in the cross-divisional development behind that distribution. Beyond InnerSource, his research interests span operating systems, distributed systems, and dynamically reconfigurable systems. He chairs the Technical Steering Committee (TSC) of the Civil Infrastructure Platform (CIP) project and serves on the Board of Directors of The Linux Foundation.

**Yuji Sanada** (Hidden Barriers to InnerSource)


Yuji Sanada works in Mitsubishi Electric's Open Source & InnerSource Program Office (OSPO), where he is involved in developing strategy and company-wide initiatives for Open Source and InnerSource. His work focuses on scaling InnerSource across diverse business domains, connecting engineering practices with corporate strategy, and promoting cross-organisational knowledge sharing and value co-creation.
He is also involved in building the organisational and technical foundations needed to expand InnerSource across the company, including community development, platform strategy, and collaboration with relevant corporate functions. Through these activities, Yuji explores how large and complex organisations can overcome silos and create new value across business boundaries.
His goal is to strengthen software development capabilities and cross-organisational co-creation in manufacturing through Open Source, InnerSource, and AI-enabled knowledge sharing.
</div>
